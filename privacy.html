Privacy

Last updated 12 November 2026

Clueboard has no accounts, no sign-in, no advertising and no tracking. While a game is running it holds the team names and what students type into it. When the game is closed, that is deleted.

Who runs Clueboard

Clueboard is run by one person, not a company.

Ben Osborne
btosborne@scu.edu

Write to that address about anything on this page, or to have something deleted, whether you are a student, a parent, a teacher or a school. It reaches the person who runs the service.

What a game puts on the network

A game room is a four-letter code. While that room is open, five things are stored in it, so that the board at the front of the class, the teacher's screen and the students' own devices can show them:

  • the team name a team types in
  • whatever a team types into the team members box
  • the answers and wagers that team locks in, and the moment it buzzed
  • that team's score
  • which clue is on the projector right now, with a copy of its picture if it has one, and its answer once the teacher reveals it

That is the whole list of what anybody types. There is no account, no password, no email address, no surname field, no class list, no age and no date of birth, because there is nowhere in Clueboard to enter one.

One thing is collected without being typed: joining a game means your device's IP address is seen by Google, whose database carries the room. Nothing else happens in the background - no analytics, no advertising identifier, no location, no fingerprinting of your device.

What never goes on the network

  • The clue set. A teacher writes their questions and answers in their own browser, or opens or pastes them there, and they stay on that device, pictures and all, and in the file they save, which goes wherever they put it. They are not uploaded. The only thing published to a room is the clue currently on the projector - its words, and a smaller copy of its picture when the clue is set to show on student devices - and its answer once the teacher reveals it, all of which the whole room is looking at anyway. The picture goes when the clue does. A video is never published: the projector finds it in its own copy of the game.
  • Anything for us. No analytics, no advertising, no tracking cookies. The only code loaded from anywhere other than this site is Google's Firebase library, which is what talks to the database - and, on the teacher's own screens only, the YouTube or Google Drive player for a video a teacher put in a clue.

Who else is involved

Google, and nobody else. The live game is carried by Google Firebase Realtime Database, and the library that talks to it is served by Google from www.gstatic.com. So a room's contents, and the IP address of every device in it, pass through Google's servers for as long as the game is running. What Google does with that is covered by Google's privacy policy.

A video in a clue plays in YouTube's privacy-enhanced player or Google Drive's own, on the projector and nowhere else, so Google sees that screen's address as it plays. A picture a teacher links to from another website, rather than putting in the game, is fetched from that website by the teacher's own screens, which that site can see. Student devices load neither: a picture reaches them as a copy inside the room, like the clue's words.

There is no analytics provider, no advertising network and no other vendor. Nothing is handed to anybody else, for any reason, unless the law requires it.

How long any of it lasts

Nothing is kept indefinitely, and there is no copy of it anywhere else. Nothing is archived, backed up by us, or looked at after the lesson.

  • Closing the room deletes it at once. Close the room on the teacher's screen wipes the teams, the scores and the game, there and then.
  • A room nobody closed stops working after an hour. Nobody can join it, the teams on it are told the game has ended, and the code goes back in the pool. It is wiped before it is handed to anybody else, so one class's room can never turn up in another's. Either way the contents are deleted the next time that teacher opens the control screen, for every room they left open and not just the last one.
  • Sooner, if you ask. Write to the address above and it will be deleted.

Your own browser remembers one thing, on your device and nowhere else, so that a refresh in the middle of a lesson does not lose your place: which room and team you are, on a student device, and the game being hosted, with its pictures, on the teacher's. Clearing your browser data clears it.

What is never done with it

Nothing from a room is sold, rented or shared. None of it is used for advertising of any kind, targeted or otherwise. None of it is used to build a profile of any student. None of it is used for any commercial purpose at all: Clueboard is free, has nothing to sell and no business model to feed.

Who can see a room, and what protects it

Anyone holding the four-letter code can read that room while it is open. There are no accounts, so the code is the only thing in the way of it. Codes are put up on the board at the front of the room and stop working when the game ends.

Everything is encrypted in transit: the site is served over HTTPS and the game connects over a secure WebSocket. What is deliberately not there is a login, so a room is only as private as its code, and nothing worth protecting should be typed into one. The clue answers are held back: each one reaches the database only when the teacher reveals it, which is the moment it goes up on the projector in front of everybody anyway.

Children's privacy, and COPPA

Clueboard is built for a classroom and collects about as little as a game played on several devices can.

What a student does type in is a team name and a team members box, which is there so the teacher can tell one team from another. If a student types a first name there, that is personal information, and it is treated as such: it goes up on the board in front of the class, it is used for nothing else, it is never shared, and it is deleted with the room. Teachers: ask for first names or a team nickname.

Nothing else about a student is asked for anywhere in the app - no surname, no email address, no age, no photograph, no location.

For schools and districts

Where students are under 13, Clueboard relies on the school's authorisation rather than writing to parents one at a time, which is the route the FTC sets out for services used in class. Two things come with that route, and Clueboard holds to both:

  • The decision belongs to the school or the district, not to an individual teacher. The FTC is explicit about this, and so are we.
  • It covers the lesson and nothing else. Nothing from a room is ever used commercially, which is the condition the whole route rests on.

On request from a school, a parent or a guardian, we will say what is held for a given room, let it be reviewed, delete it, and stop any further collection. Write to the address above. In practice a teacher closing the room does the deleting immediately, and faster than we can.

Clueboard is not a student record system and holds no education records. There is no roster, no grade book and nothing that outlives the lesson, so a school's own FERPA obligations sit where they already sit.

Other law that covers this

COPPA is American, and a page on the open web opens anywhere. Three other regimes cover the same ground:

  • The GDPR is the European one, and its Article 8 is the nearest thing to COPPA. It puts the age at which a child can agree for themselves at 16, and lets each member state lower it as far as 13 but no further. It applies where a service is offered directly to children and leans on their consent to do it - which is not this shape: Clueboard is handed to a class by a teacher, and the school is the one deciding, exactly as described above.
  • The UK's Children's Code reaches services likely to be accessed by children in the UK, educational ones included, whoever runs them and wherever they are. What it asks for is data minimisation, no profiling, no nudging, and defaults set in the child's favour rather than the service's.
  • US state student privacy law, of which California's SOPIPA is the model several others follow. It bars targeted advertising at students, building a profile of them for anything but school, and selling their information. Clueboard does none of the three, and says so above.

Rather than claim a legal reading of any of them, the useful answer is the one the rest of this page already gives. There are no accounts, no tracking, no profiling, no advertising and nothing sold or handed on, and a room's contents go when the game ends. The rights those laws grant - to be told what is held, to see it, to have it deleted, to object - are honoured for anyone who asks, wherever they are. In practice the teacher closing the room has already done the deleting.
